My day job is classifying vehicles out of recorded traffic video to Brazil's federal standard, lane by lane, in fifteen-minute intervals. It is the kind of work that punishes a bad interface immediately: look away to read a label and you lose the vehicle. So I built the interface, and then I built a second system to check my own accuracy against it.

01

A heads-up display for counting traffic

This is the real thing, rebuilt here at its true size — 360 × 400, sitting at 90% opacity over a video player so the road stays visible underneath. Every colour below is the colour it actually is in the tool, and every element answers an error I actually made in production, not a feature idea.

The active movement is always on screen

At accelerated playback it is easy to lose track of which movement you are counting. Putting it on the panel turns that from something you remember into something you glance at — and a glance costs far less than a recount.

History is colour-coded

When you rewind thirty seconds to check whether a vehicle was registered, the recount and the history have to line up immediately. Four vehicle families, four fixed hues: once the hue is learned the operator stops reading words. Reading costs a fixation; recognising a colour does not.

The last class registered stays visible

Identical vehicles arrive in runs, and re-deciding a class you just decided is wasted work. Showing the last entry removes the redundant reclassification.

One colour for state, and a line that stays empty

Mode is the only thing that changes what a key means, so it is the only thing allowed to be yellow. Nothing decorative was permitted in the warning line, so when red appears the eye goes straight to it — an always-on status bar would have made errors invisible.

02

Then I built the thing that grades me

A counting tool that is fast and wrong is worse than a slow one. So the second system is a detector — Python, YOLO, ByteTrack, CUDA — that watches the same footage and disagrees with me on purpose. Every disagreement surfaces in a web review tool as a cropped frame and one decision for a human to make.

Behind it sits the part I am most attached to, because nobody asks for it and it is what makes any accuracy claim survive an audit: a reference set of 50,374 vehicles verified by hand across 456 lane-hours. It is what turns "about 95% accurate" into a number broken out by lane, by hour of day, and by vehicle class — which is where it stops being flattering. The error is not spread evenly. It concentrates in one class, and in the hours of low sun.

03

What a day's average hides

Twelve lane-hours at one counting point, each one measured against a hand-verified count. The detector's median error across the day is +13% — a number you could put in a brochure. The chart is the reason I don't.

Nothing changes between those points — same detector, same settings, same road. What changes is the sun. From mid-afternoon it comes in low and flat down the carriageway, and the detector starts losing vehicles it had been finding all morning. By 16:00 one lane-hour is missing 99% of the traffic.

Two of twelve lane-hours actually land inside the band. A single daily average would have reported none of this, and I would have shipped it. That is why the reference set is broken out by hour and by class rather than totalled — an average is a place for an error to hide.
